# How do you treat ROS1-rearranged metastatic NSCLC?

> ✅ **Verified by jim.chen on 2026-06-07.** Auto-ingested from an internet search; reviewed and confirmed.

The primary treatment for ROS1-rearranged metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is targeted therapy using oral ROS1 tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [search-sourced]. 

Current clinical guidelines, including those from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), recommend several TKIs as preferred first-line options [search-sourced]:

* **Repotrectinib:** FDA-approved in late 2023, repotrectinib is a highly preferred first-line agent due to its strong efficacy, excellent central nervous system (CNS) activity, and secondary resistance coverage [search-sourced].
* **Entrectinib:** Another highly preferred next-generation TKI, particularly favored for patients with baseline brain metastases due to its robust intracranial activity [search-sourced].
* **Crizotinib:** A first-generation TKI that remains a standard approved first-line option [search-sourced]. Crizotinib is also utilized in ALK-positive NSCLC, though it is generally less preferred than newer TKIs in that setting [[how-do-you-treat-alk-positive-metastatic-nsclc-in-the-first-line]].

In addition to these preferred agents, newer approvals and alternative TKIs are part of the treatment landscape:
* **Taletrectinib:** FDA-approved in mid-2025, taletrectinib has emerged as a strong first-line choice due to its high efficacy and safety profile, as well as an effective second-line option for patients who progress on entrectinib or crizotinib [search-sourced].
* **Ceritinib:** A second-generation TKI that is occasionally used as an alternative agent for ROS1 fusions [search-sourced], similar to its use as a secondary option in ALK-positive NSCLC [[how-do-you-treat-alk-positive-metastatic-nsclc-in-the-first-line]].

Standard chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors are generally not preferred as frontline therapies for ROS1-rearranged NSCLC [search-sourced]. This is because patients with ROS1 fusions typically exhibit low PD-L1 expression and lower tumor mutational burdens, meaning they derive less benefit from immunotherapy compared to targeted TKIs [search-sourced]. However, if a patient is treated with crizotinib in the first line and subsequently progresses, next-line options can include repotrectinib or platinum-based doublet chemotherapy [search-sourced].

Finally, much like the standard of care for other oncogenic drivers (such as ALK fusions), guidelines emphasize that comprehensive molecular biomarker testing must ideally be completed prior to initiating first-line systemic therapy to accurately identify targets like ROS1 and ensure the proper agent is selected [[how-do-you-treat-alk-positive-metastatic-nsclc-in-the-first-line]] [search-sourced].
