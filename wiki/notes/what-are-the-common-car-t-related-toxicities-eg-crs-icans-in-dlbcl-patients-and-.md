# What are the common CAR-T related toxicities (e.g., CRS, ICANS) in DLBCL patients, and what are the recommended manageme

> ✅ **Verified by jim.chen on 2026-06-08.** Auto-ingested from an internet search; reviewed and confirmed.

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy is a highly effective treatment for relapsed or refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), but it is associated with unique, potentially severe immune-mediated toxicities [search-sourced]. The two most prominent adverse events are Cytokine Release Syndrome (CRS) and Immune Effector Cell-Associated Neurotoxicity Syndrome (ICANS) [search-sourced].

### **1. Cytokine Release Syndrome (CRS)**
**Presentation:**
CRS is an escalated systemic inflammatory response triggered by the massive release of cytokines (such as IL-6 and IFN-γ) when CAR T-cells attack target lymphoma cells [search-sourced]. It typically presents within the first 14 days post-infusion [search-sourced]. Symptoms range from mild fever and myalgias to life-threatening hypotension, hypoxia, and multiorgan failure [search-sourced]. 

**Management Strategies:**
Treatment is generally tailored to the severity of the symptoms using standard grading systems, primarily the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) consensus criteria [search-sourced]. 
*   **Grade 1 (Mild):** Management usually involves supportive care, including antipyretics for fever and intravenous fluids [search-sourced].
*   **Grade 2 to Grade 4 (Moderate to Severe):** Tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor antagonist, is the cornerstone of therapy for CRS presenting with fluid-responsive hypotension or hypoxia [search-sourced]. As CRS increases in severity (e.g., requiring vasopressors or aggressive oxygen support), systemic corticosteroids such as dexamethasone are added [search-sourced]. 
*   **Refractory CRS:** For CRS that does not respond to front-line tocilizumab and steroids, secondary agents such as anakinra (an IL-1 receptor antagonist), siltuximab, or ruxolitinib are increasingly utilized [search-sourced]. 

### **2. Immune Effector Cell-Associated Neurotoxicity Syndrome (ICANS)**
**Presentation:**
ICANS is a toxic encephalopathic state that can occur concurrently with CRS or after CRS has resolved [search-sourced]. Early clinical manifestations include tremor, dysgraphia (difficulty writing), and expressive dysphasia (difficulty speaking) [search-sourced]. In severe cases, it can rapidly progress to seizures, depressed level of consciousness, and life-threatening cerebral edema [search-sourced].

**Management Strategies:**
Like CRS, ICANS management is risk-adapted and relies on rigorous neurological assessments [search-sourced]. 
*   **Corticosteroids:** Systemic corticosteroids (e.g., dexamethasone or high-dose methylprednisolone) are the mainstay of treatment for ICANS [search-sourced]. 
*   **Tocilizumab Warnings:** While highly effective for CRS, tocilizumab does not adequately cross the blood-brain barrier [search-sourced]. Consequently, it does not resolve ICANS and may actually worsen neurotoxicity by transiently increasing IL-6 levels in the cerebrospinal fluid [search-sourced]. Management of severe ICANS typically takes precedence over CRS management [search-sourced].
*   **Seizure Management:** Anti-epileptic drugs, such as levetiracetam, are widely used for seizure prophylaxis during CAR-T therapy and for active management if seizures occur [search-sourced].

### **General Care and Prevention**
Early intervention and close monitoring in a multidisciplinary clinical setting are critical [search-sourced]. Many institutions utilize prophylactic approaches, including low-dose dexamethasone or early intervention protocols based on the timing of first fever, to arrest the progression of toxicities to life-threatening grades [search-sourced].
