# Efficacy — how to evaluate it

> **Principle node.** This page is a *reasoning lens*, not a fact sheet. When an
> entity (a drug or trial) links here, read it as "how to think about whether this
> treatment actually helps, and whom it helps." It encodes judgment, not
> sourceable claims.

## How to think about efficacy

Treatments are approved on the **statistical significance and effect size of a
defined endpoint** — e.g. an improvement in overall survival (OS) of *X* months,
or a hazard ratio for progression-free survival (PFS). Significance alone is not
the question; the question is whether the effect is **clinically meaningful** for
a real patient.

When appraising an efficacy claim, work through:

1. **What is the endpoint, and does it matter?**
   - OS is the gold standard — patients live longer.
   - PFS / DFS / EFS are surrogates: faster to read out, but a PFS gain does not
     always translate to living longer or feeling better. Weigh accordingly.
   - ORR / response rate is a signal of activity, not of benefit.
   - Be explicit about which endpoint the approval rests on.

2. **How big is the effect, in absolute terms?**
   - A hazard ratio of 0.7 sounds strong, but translate it: *how many months* of
     median OS/PFS, and what does the curve separation look like at the tail?
   - Distinguish a statistically significant but marginal gain (weeks) from a
     practice-changing one (durable plateau, cure fraction).

3. **Who was actually studied?**
   - Trial populations are selected (good PS, limited comorbidity). Ask whether
     your patient would have been eligible.

4. **Subset and post-hoc analyses — signal, not proof.**
   - A trial is powered for its primary endpoint, not its subgroups. A positive
     subset (e.g. benefit concentrated in PD-L1–high, or in a biomarker subgroup)
     is a **hypothesis-generating signal** that specific populations may benefit
     more — or that others may not benefit at all.
   - Treat subset findings as a steer for the *next* question, not as established
     fact. Look for consistency across trials before acting on them.

5. **Identify who benefits most — and who doesn't.**
   - The clinically useful read of a paper is rarely "the drug works." It is
     "*this* subset derives most of the benefit, and *that* subset derives little,
     so I would favor it here and not there."

## When a drug or trial links here, ask

- Which endpoint anchors the benefit, and is it OS or a surrogate?
- What is the **absolute** gain (months, % at a landmark, tail plateau)?
- Is the benefit uniform, or concentrated in a definable subset?
- Would my patient resemble the trial population?
- Does the magnitude justify the [[adverse-events|toxicity]] and burden?
