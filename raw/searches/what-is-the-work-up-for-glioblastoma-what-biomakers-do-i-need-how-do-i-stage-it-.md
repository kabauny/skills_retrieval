# What is the work up for glioblastoma? What biomakers do I need. How do I stage it, what is the initial therapy? 

Based on current clinical guidelines, including those from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the World Health Organization (WHO) 2021 Classification of Central Nervous System Tumors, here is the clinical approach for a patient with glioblastoma:

### Workup
The initial diagnostic workup for a suspected glioblastoma includes:
*   **Imaging:** A comprehensive brain MRI with and without gadolinium contrast is the gold standard to evaluate the size, location, and infiltrative nature of the tumor [search-sourced]. 
*   **Surgical Tissue Acquisition:** Maximal safe surgical resection or a stereotactic biopsy is necessary to obtain tissue for accurate histopathological and molecular diagnosis [search-sourced]. 
*   **Post-Operative Imaging:** A post-operative MRI is typically performed within 24 to 48 hours after surgery to assess the extent of the tumor resection [search-sourced].

### Required Biomarkers
Under the updated 2021 WHO classification, molecular diagnostics are essential for diagnosing and classifying glioblastoma [search-sourced]. The key biomarkers you need to test for include:
*   **IDH (Isocitrate Dehydrogenase) Mutation Status:** A true glioblastoma is now strictly defined as *IDH-wildtype* [search-sourced]. Tumors that are IDH-mutant are now classified as astrocytomas, even if they are grade 4 [search-sourced].
*   **MGMT Promoter Methylation:** Testing for MGMT promoter methylation status is crucial [search-sourced]. It is a predictive biomarker; tumors with a methylated MGMT promoter generally respond much better to the chemotherapy drug temozolomide [search-sourced].
*   **GBM Defining Alterations:** In the absence of traditional grade 4 histological features (like necrosis or microvascular proliferation), an IDH-wildtype diffuse glioma is still diagnosed as a glioblastoma if it possesses one or more of the following molecular hallmarks: **TERT promoter mutation**, **EGFR amplification**, or **combined chromosome 7 gain and chromosome 10 loss (+7/-10)** [search-sourced]. 

### Staging
Unlike most systemic cancers, brain tumors like glioblastomas do not use the traditional TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) staging system because they rarely spread outside the central nervous system [search-sourced]. Instead, they are evaluated using the WHO grading system [search-sourced]. Glioblastoma is inherently a **WHO Grade 4** tumor, meaning it is a highly malignant, fast-growing, and aggressive cancer right from the beginning [search-sourced].

### Initial Therapy
Because full removal of these highly invasive tumors is generally not possible, a multimodal treatment approach is standard [search-sourced]:
*   **Surgery:** The first step is maximal safe surgical resection, aiming to remove as much of the tumor as possible without causing severe neurological deficits [search-sourced].
*   **Concurrent Chemoradiation (The Stupp Protocol):** For patients with good performance status, the standard of care following surgery is 6 weeks of fractionated focal radiotherapy (typically delivering 50 to 60 Gy) concurrent with daily oral temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy [search-sourced].
*   **Adjuvant Chemotherapy:** After completing the radiation phase, patients typically receive adjuvant (maintenance) temozolomide for 6 cycles [search-sourced].
*   **Elderly or Frail Patients:** According to NCCN guidelines, patients who are older (e.g., >70 years) or have a poor performance status may receive hypofractionated radiation therapy (a shorter, more concentrated course of radiation) with or without temozolomide, depending on their MGMT methylation status [search-sourced].
*   **Clinical Trials:** The NCCN highly recommends that eligible patients be considered for enrollment in clinical trials due to the challenging prognosis of the disease [search-sourced].
